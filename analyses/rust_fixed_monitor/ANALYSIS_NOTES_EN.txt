Recording comparison

We repeated the paired recording comparison in TEP-Rust, a separate implementation of the driver and controllers derived from the same Braatz Fortran code. With the fixed historical Fault 9 monitor, recording the measurements used by the fast controller detected 70 of 75 runs cleanly within 40 h, compared with 21 using the standard Rust output recorded one model evaluation later (1 s); each recording raised alarms in 2 of 60 normal evaluation runs (SI Section [X]).

Methods and results

We ran the official TEP Studio WebAssembly module using closed-loop Euler integration at a 1-s step for 48 h. Its fast controllers act every third step and read the measurements from the preceding step. The standard recorder returns the new measurements from the current model evaluation with the valve commands for that step. At steps 180, 360, …, 172800, we therefore paired the same eleven valve commands with either the 41 controller-input measurements or the 41 measurements returned by the standard recorder. The two records differ by one evaluation; their valve commands, physical trajectory, control calculations and random draws are the same. A separate default-cadence replay matched all 960 × 52 native values for one normal and one fault run. Only IDV(9) was scheduled, at 8 h on fault runs; the optional forced IDV(12) was disabled. All 194 calibration and evaluation runs returned the simulator status “completed” and produced 960 records.

The historical affine Fault 9 score and signed two-sided CUSUM (k=0.05) were unchanged. Each recording received its own limit, the 57th in ascending order among 59 normal calibration-run maxima (the third-highest), giving limits of 29.34 for controller-input and 77.75 for native recording. On 60 further normal runs, both monitors alarmed in 2/60, on different runs. Normal score variances were 0.995 and 2.387, respectively. Of the 75 fault runs, 21 were detected cleanly by both within 40 h, 49 only under controller-input recording, and 5 by neither. Under the specified delay convention, measured from the first affected record (161), 22 versus 0 were detected within 8 h. Record 161 was stamped 179 s after fault injection; one controller-input run first alarmed at record 321, exactly 8 h after record 161. Counting strictly from injection therefore gave 21 versus 0 within 8 h. The 2 h (0 versus 0) and full 40 h counts were unchanged. No fault run alarmed before onset. Mean penalized losses on the specified record-based clock were 980.4 and 2170.2 min. The paired comparison is descriptive: the port retains the original scalar generator, and distinct starting seeds do not guarantee disjoint random-number streams.

Interpretation and limitations

The recording effect occurred in two drivers with different logging offsets (3 s in tep and 1 s in TEP-Rust) but a common process and controller lineage. Its size is conditional on the monitor, controller and data-generation settings; we have not measured it under the revised Ricker control structure or in plant data. Koçak et al. (2026) used the Rieth data for run-level multiclass classification, so their results cannot be attributed to post-update recording. The external evaluation in the preprint of Lyu et al. (2026) used default output from the tep interface, but excluded Faults 3, 9 and 15 and did not compare recording conventions. We have not established that timing explains any discrepancy between published detection rates.

Implementation and source limitations. For tep 2.5.0, the evidence documents observed recording behaviour; developer intent is not established. Neither the documentation nor the public issue search supplied a rationale for this pairing. The two process backends share a recording driver. Official source identifiers are listed in PUBLIC_SOURCE_BINDINGS.json. The calibration limit uses the 57th value in ascending order.
